introduction ∙ evaluating my online presence

where i stand now

If a recruiter searched my name today, they would not find me. The first page belongs to other people: a filmmaker who shares my surname and has a Wikipedia page, a director with a film in production, a police official, a Linux kernel contributor. My LinkedIn doesn’t surface, and neither does my work at VibeGrow or anything I’ve built.

The profile itself tells a similar story. I have more than 500 connections and a verified account, but my headline lists only my school, the banner is LinkedIn’s default, and there is no About section or experience. My professional reputation online, right now, is mostly an absence. That is a better problem than a bad reputation, but it is still a problem.

the brand i’m building

Over the next five years I want to be known as an engineer who builds systems that run real business operations, and who can explain them to the people who depend on them. My work on multi-agent coordination at VibeGrow is the start of that. My finance degree is the reason I care about what software is for, not only how it works. By 2031 I want someone searching my name to find a body of work: projects, writing, and products I helped ship. The brand is simple: technical depth, business sense, and the ability to connect the two.

three strategies

Own my name in search. Because my name is shared with people who already rank, I have to give search engines something unambiguous. This site will live on its own domain under my full name, and my LinkedIn, GitHub, and site will link to each other so they reinforce one identity. I will use “Charan Reddy Yakkanti” exactly the same way everywhere, so my work stops being split across variations of the name.

Make LinkedIn the hub. LinkedIn is where recruiters will look first, and mine currently says almost nothing. I will rewrite the headline to name my role and focus, add an About section that tells the story connecting my two degrees, list VibeGrow and Tabner as experience, add F1TENTH and my routing project under Projects, and replace the default banner and photo. Every document in this portfolio should say the same thing the profile says.

Post on a schedule, about real work. I will post on LinkedIn once a week, every week, about something I built, broke, or learned: a coordination problem at VibeGrow, a lesson from autonomous racing, an IBM Z session. When something ships, I will post twice that week. Once a month I will publish a longer piece in the blog on this site and share that piece on LinkedIn. The rule is that every post has to be about work I actually did, because that is the only kind of content that builds credibility rather than noise.

The first version was one explanation, and it did not hold. A reader still could not tell an agent swarm from one agent doing every job in a row. I split the writing into a blog that lives on this website, in pieces short enough to read alone and pointed enough to share. The longer monthly post in my LinkedIn plan is one of these, not a document sitting in a folder.

    Growth and sales contact the same people. The sensors on the car stop agreeing. Two competent steps, one stale board.

  • october 20, 2026 the hard part is the handoff

    The failure is not that an agent is unintelligent. The failure is that the next one acts on a decision the others have already left behind.

  • october 6, 2026 multiplayer agents share one board

    What I mean by multiplayer agents: more than one agent in the same effort, reading the same record, at the same time.

  • september 22, 2026 a swarm is not a crowd of chatbots

    An agent swarm at VibeGrow is different jobs and one objective. More copies of the same chatbot is not a swarm.

One agent doing one task is straightforward. The difficulty shows up when five of them share an objective: each needs to know what the others have already settled, and none can see the whole picture at once. The coordination layer carries that context across the handoff, and when it fails, two agents solve the same problem twice while a third works from a premise that was abandoned ten minutes ago.

./reflection

conclusion ∙ written after everything else was built

The uncomfortable discovery was how easy it is to have done the work and still not exist online. A search for my name returns other people. My LinkedIn had more than 500 connections, a verified badge, and almost none of the story: school in the headline, the default banner, no About, no experience. I had been treating a profile as something you finish once. A stranger meets whichever version of you is easiest to find, and right now that version is an absence.

Credibility turned out to be consistency rather than polish. The résumé, the CV, the SpaceX letter, and the pitch all have to describe the same person, with the audience changed and the facts left alone. The résumé leads with VibeGrow because a recruiter will not read the recruiting internship first. The CV keeps the longer project write-ups because a research reader will. The letter is aimed at one posting. Writing them side by side taught me that cutting is a skill, and that audience is a constraint, not an introduction.

The ethics lesson arrived as metadata. The elevator-pitch file I recorded still had GPS coordinates in it. I had thought about what I was saying on camera. I had not thought about what the file was publishing underneath the picture. Stripping that data before this site went up was a small edit and a real correction. A boundary includes the parts you did not mean to hand over: a location, a claim you cannot open and show, a detail that belongs to someone else. I will treat those the same way from here.

I also changed my mind about the person on the other side of an explanation. At Tabner, clients could describe a hiring goal and could not describe the role. On F1TENTH, teammates outside software needed the tradeoff, not the stack. The reader this page is for is in that same spot. They are not uninterested. They are missing the picture. That is why the agent section lets someone step through a duplicated handoff instead of trusting a paragraph about it. Explaining the work is part of the work.

For the search, the value is practical. I can send one link that holds the résumé, the CV, the letter, the pitch, and the blog, each with a note on who it is for. The posts stay up after the assignment, which is the difference between a site and a folder of files. A website still cannot do the conversation: Ronan going through a draft with me, a recruiter asking what I would do differently on the failed runs. The site makes the work findable. It does not replace showing up.

Thanks for scrolling this far. I spend most of my time on a problem that sounds small and isn’t: getting several systems to work toward one goal without losing track of each other. Turns out that’s most of engineering, and most of business too. If you’re working on something like that, I’d like to hear about it.
